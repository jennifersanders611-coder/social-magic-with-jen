I've worked in a lot of different industries throughout my career, including nonprofit, finance, energy, and legal. Along the way, I've worked under a lot of different leaders and had the opportunity to lead teams that looked very different from one organization to the next.

Different industries, different personalities, different team structures, and very different ideas about what good leadership looks like.

And one thing I've learned is that being a kind person and being a kind leader aren't necessarily the same thing.

With World Kindness Day here, I wanted to share a few things I've learned about both.

## Being nice is easy when things are going well

Most people can be kind when everything is running smoothly. When the numbers look good, the team is happy, and there aren't any difficult decisions to make.

But what about when something goes wrong?

When someone makes a mistake? When a deadline gets missed? When the business is struggling or you have to deliver news nobody wants to hear?

That's when kindness in leadership really matters.

I've worked with leaders who were genuinely nice people but struggled with difficult conversations, accountability, or communication. And I've worked with leaders who had incredibly high expectations but made you feel respected, supported, and valued even when things weren't going well.

There's a difference.

## The little things are usually the big things

When I think about the kindest leaders I've worked with, I don't immediately think about gifts, celebrations, or grand gestures.

I think about the everyday things they did.

- They took ownership when something went wrong instead of looking for someone to blame.
- They weren't afraid to say, "I don't know."
- If they made a mistake, they admitted it. If they forgot something, they owned that too.
- They gave credit to the people doing the work, especially when those people weren't in the room.
- They listened, followed through, and remembered that the people on their teams had lives outside of work.

None of those things required a budget or a company-wide initiative.

But they made a difference.

## Kindness doesn't mean avoiding accountability

This might be the biggest distinction for me.

Being a kind leader doesn't mean saying yes to everything, avoiding conflict, or making sure everyone likes you.

Sometimes kindness means having the conversation nobody wants to have.

It means being honest when expectations aren't being met, giving someone feedback they need to hear, or making a difficult decision for the good of the team.

But how you handle those moments matters.

You can hold someone accountable without embarrassing them. You can disagree without being dismissive. You can deliver difficult news without forgetting there's a person on the receiving end.

Kindness and accountability can absolutely coexist. In fact, I think the best leaders understand that you need both.

## What it's like to work for a kind leader

There's something different about working for a leader who makes you feel safe enough to ask questions, admit mistakes, and share ideas.

You spend less time worrying about how something will be received and more time figuring out how to make it better.

You don't have to pretend you know everything. You can raise a concern before it becomes a bigger problem. You can disagree respectfully without feeling like you've damaged a relationship.

And when something goes wrong, you know the conversation will be about solving the problem, not finding someone to blame.

That kind of environment doesn't happen by accident. Leaders create it through the way they show up every day.

## The kind of leader I want to be

Having worked under different leaders and led different teams myself, I know I haven't always gotten everything right.

Leadership is hard. People are complicated. Business decisions aren't always easy.

But I think kindness is something we can choose to practice, even when the circumstances aren't ideal.

For me, that means being approachable, owning my mistakes, giving people credit, communicating clearly, and remembering that the way I make someone feel matters just as much as the work we're trying to accomplish.

It also means holding myself to the same standards I expect from others.

Because being a kind leader isn't about being everyone's favorite person.

It's about being someone people can trust, especially when things get difficult.

And that's the kind of leadership that stays with people long after they've moved on to their next job.

Happy World Kindness Day! 💜
